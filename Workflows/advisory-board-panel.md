## Austin Marchese — Chair

AI agency founder; ex-COO of a $25M startup, ex-JPMorgan engineer; runs an 91.5K-subscriber channel on building with Claude (up from 89.3K on 2026-09-16, 74K before that). Practical, anti-hype, insists on receipts. As chair he structures the audit, keeps advisors evidence-bound, and writes the synthesis.

**His four rules** (the audit's backbone — run every job in scope through them):

1. **Match the bottleneck to a solution.** Identify the actual constraint before building. His five-tool menu: skills, loops, routines, artifacts, external tools. Use 5-Whys to find the root bottleneck. Audit question: *what bottleneck does this job solve, and is the tool choice right — or is it automation for its own sake?* **Over-specification is its own bottleneck** (refined 2026-09-16, from "The REAL Reason The Creator of Claude Code Told You To Delete Your Skills," full transcript read): reframing Boris Cherny's viral "delete everything" clip, his position is delete everything that *over-constrains* Claude, not literal deletion. Four shifts he now teaches: rules → judgment (a rule like "never use bullet points" becomes a standard like "write the way my last three emails read"), examples → interfaces (a rigid template becomes a guide with constraints, reserved for genuinely rigid outputs), upfront context → progressive disclosure (point Claude at where context sits instead of loading it all upfront), and memory/verification-by-default (current models now do both automatically, reducing how much used to need spelling out). Audit question, added: *is this job's rules/context calibrated to the actual need, or is the constraint itself now the bottleneck?*
2. **Proactive systems — Claude comes to you.** Every proactive system has four parts: **trigger** (time/event cadence), **worker** (does the job; minimum viable, don't solve theoretical futures), **access** (the tools/mounts it needs — like an employee's logins), **receipt** (the expected output that proves it ran). Audit question: *name all four parts for this job. A missing part is a finding.* (Grace mapping: Automation.md's "Required mounts" section is the access column; a job that "reads empty" when a mount is missing is an access failure.) *(Refined 2026-09-16, "6 EASY Steps to Automate 99% of Your Work With Claude," ~Sep 7 2026: his own compressed restatement is a "minimum viable stack — trigger, logic, monitoring" plus a discrete first step, "the automation spike" — logic ≈ worker, monitoring ≈ receipt, access folded into "how/where to run." Same framework, not a contradiction.)*
3. **Read the traces.** A receipt is the outcome summary; a trace is the full story of how it got there. Three steps: create the logs, visualize the logs, act on the logs — feeding lessons back into the skill so the fix applies to every future run. Audit question: *if this job did the wrong thing last Tuesday, how would anyone know, and how would the lesson get back into the workflow file?*
4. **Hand it objectives, not just tasks.** For bigger asks: agree on what done looks like before work starts (a granular punch list, not vibes); add a separate evaluator — the worker can't honestly grade its own output; require the receipt to come back with proof (e.g., a screenshot), not just an assertion of success. *(Reinforced 2026-09-16, "Claude's New Feature Automates Any Task," ~Aug 2026: a "3-Step Evaluator Skill" — same position, new example.)*

**How he says to write a skill** *(added 2026-09-30, "Matt Pocock's EXACT System To 10x Your Claude Skills," Sep 29 2026. Sources: the creator-written description and chapters, plus a Gemini summary of the video requested by Connor — **a summary, not a verbatim transcript, and Gemini can err**; the YouTube transcript panel wouldn't load)*. Austin's plain-English translation of how Matt Pocock builds every skill. Core idea: a skill is a correction you got tired of making. Five steps: (1) **notice what you keep sending back** — build a skill around one step of your workflow that you repeatedly correct, not a whole end-to-end process (models rush to a finished result); (2) **use leading words** — name an established term or framework (e.g. pre-mortem, steelman, refactor) instead of defining it, since the model already knows it; (3) **keep skills small and user-invoked** — a few lines can be enough, and turning off automatic model invocation saves context and avoids surprise triggers; (4) **iterate with the "send it back" test** — use it in real work, and add a line only when a real failure shows up; if you've stopped sending output back, it works; (5) **cut the no-ops** — drop filler like "be thorough" and "don't do X" negatives; say what to do. Closing position: don't copy someone else's skill files; build your own from your own friction. Extends Rule 1's over-specification point to skill authoring. Audit question, added: *does each skill trace to a repeated correction, stay small and user-invoked, and have every line change the output?* The example terms are from the Gemini summary (secondary); confirm against the video before quoting.

**His cautionary tales** (use when the panel weighs adding more automation): Ford rehired 350 engineers after its AI push fell short; Commonwealth Bank pulled its AI voice system. Failed automation is expensive — the "secret art" that separates working systems isn't technical, it's the unglamorous discipline of rules 3–4. *(Reinforced 2026-09-16, "The 5 Biggest Lies You've Been Told About Claude," ~Aug 2026: names "automate everything instantly and be hands-off" and "more agents = more output = more value" explicitly as lies — sharper language, same warning.)*

## Boris Cherny — creator of Claude Code

Austin's most-cited engineer. Known for a surprisingly "vanilla," simplicity-first setup.

**Positions:**
- **"Find the bottleneck, solve the bottleneck"** (to Fortune). The order matters: find it, solve it *manually*, and only then codify it into a skill so it never has to be faced again. Automating an unsolved problem bakes in the confusion.
- **Plan first.** Start work in plan mode; agree on the approach before execution.
- **Simplicity.** Prefer boring, vanilla mechanisms over clever ones; delete what isn't earning its keep.
- **The compounding loop.** Use a skill → inspect the output → update the skill. A skill that never gets updated after its runs isn't compounding, it's decaying.
- **Higher bar for AI code, not lower** *(added 2026-09-16, his Sept 11–12, 2026 public reply, posted on X, to a developer's "AI slop" email)*. Production code written by Claude should be held to a **stricter** standard than human-written code — enforced through layered automated guardrails (lint rules, Claude-driven e2e tests, daily fuzzers, automated code/security review, automated refactoring), not manual vigilance alone. Throwaway/low-blast-radius code is the exception, not the rule. The developer's job shifts to holding that bar, not writing every line.

**What Boris examines in Grace Brain:** for each job, was there a manual era before the automated era (the workflow changelogs usually show this)? Are the workflow files actually updated in response to run outcomes (changelog entries citing run evidence = healthy; a changelog silent since creation = decaying)? Is anything over-clever where boring would do?

## Andrej Karpathy — former Director of AI at Tesla, OpenAI founding member

Subject of Austin's biggest video ("Karpathy's Method", 660K views): **write specs, not prompts**.

**Positions:**
- **Specs over prompts.** A vague instruction produces a vague result. The spec — granular, written, agreed — is the real program; the prompt is just its delivery.
- **Keep it on a leash.** Autonomy must be earned in increments. Hand over small, verifiable chunks; expand scope only after the system proves reliable at the current one. Never delegate what you can't verify.
- **Verification is the bottleneck**, not generation. The scarce resource is Connor's ability to check work, so design outputs to be cheap to verify.
- **Spec beats plan mode** *(added 2026-09-16, sourced, paraphrase-level confidence)*. Even Claude Code's own plan mode is too shallow on its own — push further into a detailed, agreed written spec before delegating.
- **The leash, operationalized** *(added 2026-09-16, flagged as extrapolation — secondary source, not a confirmed direct quote)*: write a verifier, cap the retry budget, put the task on a trigger, and only widen autonomy once the verifier proves reliable.

**What Karpathy examines in Grace Brain:** do the workflow files read as specs (exact steps, defined outputs, edge cases) or as vibes? Is each job's autonomy proportionate to its track record — e.g., a brand-new job writing directly to trackers vs. an established one? Are receipts designed for cheap verification (counts, diffs, screenshots) or do they require re-doing the work to check it? Ask-first gates on destructive/irreversible steps are his kind of leash — flag anywhere that lacks one.

## Jeff Su — ex-Google product marketing manager, productivity & AI-workflows creator (1.2M+ subscribers)

Built the **CORE Workflow** (Capture → Organize → Review → Engage) and taught it to 6,642 Googlers over nine years, drawing on Getting Things Done, Make Time, and Building a Second Brain. Explicitly platform-agnostic — the four-phase loop is the point, not any particular tool.

**Positions:**
- **Four types of workplace information — Tasks, Ideas, Notes, Media — and each needs a defined home.** A system that captures well but has nowhere organized to put things just moves the clutter around. **Applied to AI specifically** *(added 2026-09-16, "My AI Remembers Everything," Aug 12 2026)*: memory has the same three-tier structure — global (automatic, account-wide), project-scoped, and a custom system you build yourself only when the first two aren't enough. Building your own is a deliberate choice tied to work complexity, not a default.
- **Review is the phase everyone skips**, and it's the one that keeps the whole system honest. Without a Review step, Capture quietly turns into a graveyard.
- **Engage closes the loop.** Information that's been captured, organized, and reviewed but never acted on hasn't actually been "handled" — it's been filed.
- **Tool-agnostic by design.** The workflow compounds, not the software. A new plugin or a tool swap becoming the project is itself a warning sign.

**What Jeff examines in Grace Brain:** for each job, map it against Capture/Organize/Review/Engage — is any phase silently missing? (A job that captures into a tracker but has no scheduled Review — e.g., the Closure Candidates queue, if nothing is confirming or dismissing entries on a cadence — is Capture without Review.) Does Engage actually happen: do items in Open Actions close, or accumulate indefinitely? Is Connor's toolchain (Obsidian, Rock, Outlook, iMessage) being run as one CORE loop, or as four disconnected ones that each capture but don't hand off to each other?

*(Extrapolation note: Jeff's CORE framework is built for individual knowledge work, not multi-source automation systems like this vault. His lens is being applied by analogy — mark any finding built on this stretch explicitly.)*

## Dylan Davis — AI automation consultant ("AI Whisperer," Gradient Labs), @dylandavisAI

Coaches teams on which AI automations are actually worth building — framed as "here's how this saves you four hours a week," not "look what AI can do." Public position, via LinkedIn: **"your AI agent is probably over-engineered."**

**Positions:**
- **ROI first.** Every automation should answer a specific hours-saved or error-avoided question before it gets built. Novelty and hype aren't a business case.
- **Over-engineering is the default failure mode**, not under-engineering. Most AI agents do more than the job needs; the fix is usually to cut scope, not add capability.
- **AI drafts, you direct, you verify.** In a working pipeline, AI handles first-pass or repetitive work while a human keeps strategy, review, and the final call — never full autonomy without a checkpoint.

**What Dylan examines in Grace Brain:** for each job, is there a stated hours-saved or error-avoided rationale — Automation.md doesn't currently track this, which is itself a finding — or was it built because it seemed doable? Which jobs carry more branching logic or scope than their actual bottleneck justifies? Where AI drafts something a human is supposed to check (email replies, form fills, synthesis summaries), is there a real verify-and-direct checkpoint, or does the output flow straight through unread?

*(Extrapolation note, updated 2026-09-16: his channel now carries a dense, sustained run of directly on-topic videos — a real throughline, not a handful of posts, so "thin" no longer describes his record accurately. Still no single named framework like Jeff Su's CORE, so treat his positions as directional rather than a settled methodology, but not as lightly evidenced as this note originally said.)*

## Andy Stanley — founder, North Point Ministries

Pastor and author of *What Great Leaders Do* and other leadership books; hosts the long-running Andy Stanley Leadership Podcast. Grace Community Church is a **North Point Partner Church** — meaning Grace's own leadership structure has a real, documented lineage to Stanley's systems, not just a thematic resemblance.

**Positions:**
- **The Four Levels of Delegation** (Investigation → Informed Progress → Informed Results → Ownership): oversight should shrink as a person or system proves reliable at the current level — delegation is a graduated process, not a single on/off switch.
- **Differentiate problems to solve from tensions to manage.** Some issues get fixed once; others are permanent trade-offs that recur no matter how well the last round was handled, and treating a tension as a solvable problem just produces repeated, frustrated "fixes."
- **Systems over heroics.** An organization needs a repeatable way to pull the right people and information to a decision — not dependence on one person's memory, initiative, or constant personal attention.
- **Delegation compounds through trust, not just structure** *(added 2026-09-16, a 2026 podcast conversation with his longtime executive assistant Diane Grant)*. Saying "you decide" — handing over a decision, not just a task — is itself a leadership move once trust is earned; it multiplies what a leader can carry rather than diluting control.
- **Delegate by strength-fit as well as readiness** *(added 2026-09-16, "Great Leaders Play to Their Strengths, Part 1," Sept 7 2026)*. A second axis alongside the Four Levels' track-record test: identify where you personally add the most value and hand off what someone else can do better, not only what's already proven reliable.

**What Stanley examines in Grace Brain:** whether each job's autonomy tracks an actual delegation level rather than a flat default — e.g., the m365 sync's Run Log is "Informed Progress" evidence that could justify moving it toward "Informed Results," while a brand-new job has no business starting anywhere but "Investigation." He'd also ask the panel to sort its own recurring frictions into the two buckets: a missing Run Log is a solvable problem; Cat-vs-Dylan (more routines vs. justify the ROI first) is a tension to manage indefinitely, not a disagreement waiting for a winner. *(No genuine tension with Karpathy: the Four Levels of Delegation reinforces his "leash earned in increments" position rather than contesting it — the panel notes this as alignment, not manufactured friction.)*

## Panel dynamics

Real tensions to let surface rather than suppress:

- **Austin vs. Karpathy** — Austin's instinct is to automate the next bottleneck; Karpathy's is to slow down until verification catches up. Both are right at different maturity stages; the disagreement section exists for exactly this.
- **Cat vs. Boris** — Cat looks for what else could become a routine; Boris looks for what should be deleted or simplified. A healthy audit usually contains both a "consider adding" and a "consider removing."
- **Ash vs. everyone** — Ash will trade feature work for observability every time. When the panel proposes anything new, Ash's standing question is "and what's its trace?"
- **Cat vs. Dylan** — Cat pushes for more surface area: more routines, more cadences, less for Connor to chase by hand. Dylan pushes the other way: justify the hours saved before adding anything. Neither is wrong; a job that clears Cat's "is this earned" test should still clear Dylan's "what's the ROI" test, and the panel should say so explicitly when they land on opposite sides of a proposal.
- **Stanley → Slye (inheritance, not tension)** — Grace is a North Point Partner Church, so Slye's own leadership systems descend from Stanley's; when Slye's lens surfaces a concern about the system losing sight of real relationships, Stanley's delegation framework is a plausible existing tool for addressing it, not a competing view.
- **Slye vs. the rest of the panel** — every other advisor evaluates the automation from outside; Slye is the one person whose actual ministry the system is built to serve. When his "is this still about real people" question conflicts with a design-quality finding from Austin, Karpathy, or Cat, that's a genuine tension worth keeping, not resolving in the write-up.
